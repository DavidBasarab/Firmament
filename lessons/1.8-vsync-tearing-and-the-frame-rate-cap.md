# Lesson 1.8 — VSync, Tearing, and the Frame Rate Cap

> Part 1 — A Window and a Heartbeat
> *VSync, tearing, and why your FPS counter says 60 no matter what you do*

**Where you already are:** `FirmamentWindow` opens a D3D11 host, rebuilds its swap chain and viewport on resize, cycles a clear color, and reports FPS / avg ms / peak ms / updates / resizes / size / aspect in the title bar. It ends every frame with one line:

```csharp
swapChain.Present(0, 0);
```

Two zeros, never explained, and they are the two most consequential numbers in the file. This lesson explains both.

**A correction to the curriculum line above, before anything else.** "Why your FPS counter says 60 no matter what you do" was written against Windows 8.1-era behavior, and it does not describe your machine. Run what you have right now and the title bar reads something like **10,868 FPS | 0.09 ms avg** on a ~60 Hz panel. That is correct and expected — the `0` you passed really is buying you an uncapped render rate. What it is *not* buying you is 10,868 images on the glass. Your display is still showing 60. The gap between those two numbers is the actual subject of this lesson, and it's a more interesting one than the title promised.

**Divergences from the 1.7 doc, since you tweaked it (as expected):**

- You named the methods `OnFrameBufferResize` and `SetViewPort`. The D3D type is `Viewport` and the Silk event is `FramebufferResize` — both are single words in graphics parlance, so the house naming rule points at `OnFramebufferResize` / `SetViewport`. Cosmetic, and I'll use *your* names in every diff below so you can paste without thinking.
- `RSSetViewports(1, ref viewport)` instead of `in viewport`. Identical call; `in` just promises you won't write to it. Leave it.
- Your IDE re-sorted the field block alphabetically and scattered blank lines through it. Harmless. CSharpier doesn't reorder members, so that was ReSharper.
- Title-bar field order moved around. Doesn't matter — but note you now have **seven** readouts in there. It's getting long. **5.8** puts text on the screen and this whole string goes away.
- `OnRender` still calls `OMSetRenderTargets` every single frame. Redundant in a vacuum (the binding is sticky), but it is exactly what makes 1.7's unbind-before-resize necessary, and it will stop being redundant the moment 7.1 introduces a second render target. Keep it.

## Concept: a monitor is a scan line with a deadline

Your display does not "show a frame." It redraws itself continuously, one row of pixels at a time, top to bottom, reading out of whatever memory it has been pointed at. At 60 Hz it finishes a full pass every 16.67 ms, then blanks briefly and starts over. That gap is the **vertical blanking interval** — **vblank** — and it is the only instant at which swapping which buffer the display reads from is invisible.

Swap in the middle of a scanout and the top of the screen is still showing the old frame while the bottom shows the new one. The seam between them is **tearing**. It is not corruption; it's two correct images stacked. It's most visible on horizontal camera pans, which is why it feels like a 3D-game problem and not a 2D one.

**VSync** is the agreement to only ever swap during vblank. You get a whole, coherent image every time. The cost is *waiting*: if you finish a frame 1 ms after vblank, you sit on it for 15.67 ms. Miss the deadline by a hair at 60 Hz and you don't drop to 58 FPS, you drop to 30 — the next opportunity is a full refresh away. That cliff is why "just turn on VSync" isn't a free win.

The first argument to `Present` is the **sync interval**: *how many vblanks to wait before this frame becomes visible.*

| Value | Meaning | Result on a 60 Hz panel |
|---|---|---|
| `0` | Don't wait for vblank | uncapped **render** rate, still a 60 Hz **display** rate — see below |
| `1` | Wait for the next vblank | 60 FPS ceiling, no tearing |
| `2` | Wait for the *second* vblank | 30 FPS ceiling, no tearing |
| `3`, `4` | …and so on | 20, 15 |

### Rendered frames vs. displayed frames

Your swap chain uses the **flip model** (`SwapEffect.FlipDiscard` — 1.5 chose it, and this is the moment it matters). In the flip model your back buffer isn't *copied* to the screen: the Desktop Window Manager is handed the texture and told to display it directly. DWM composites the desktop **once per vblank**, and it has no mechanism to show you a frame between refreshes. So no matter what you pass to `Present`, **60 images per second reach the glass on a 60 Hz panel.** That number is not yours to change.

The question is what happens to the frames you produce faster than that, and it depends on which flip effect you chose:

| | Sync interval `0`, `BufferCount = 2` |
|---|---|
| `FlipSequential` (Win 8.1) | Every present must be queued for a vblank. With two buffers the queue is full after one frame, so the second `Present` **blocks** until DWM releases a buffer — at vblank. "Don't wait for vblank" waited for vblank, and your counter read 60. |
| `FlipDiscard` (Win 10+) | The word is *discard*. A present that arrives before the previous one has been shown doesn't queue behind it, it **replaces** it. The un-shown frame is thrown away and your thread returns immediately. Uncapped. |

You are on the second row. That is why you read five figures on a solid-color clear, and it's why the chapter title is a lie on any modern machine — `FlipDiscard` is precisely the Windows 10 fix for the behavior that title describes.

So, honestly: at ~10,000 FPS on a 60 Hz panel you are **rendering** ten thousand frames per second and **displaying** sixty. Fewer than one frame in every 160 you draw is ever seen by a human. Nothing is capped — and nothing is torn either, because DWM is still compositing once per vblank and simply taking whichever frame is current when it looks.

That last clause is the thing left to buy. An uncapped render rate and a composited display rate is what you already have; what you *don't* have is any way to get a frame onto the scanout between refreshes.

**`DXGI_PRESENT_ALLOW_TEARING`** is that escape hatch — added in DXGI 1.5 (Windows 10), alongside `FLIP_DISCARD` itself. It tells DXGI to bypass composition and hand the frame straight to scanout, mid-scan if necessary. That's the second `Present` argument, and it comes with three rules the runtime enforces on pain of `DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL`:

1. The swap chain must have been **created** with `DXGI_SWAP_CHAIN_FLAG_ALLOW_TEARING`. Opt-in at creation, not at present.
2. The sync interval must be **`0`**. "Wait for vblank, but also tear" is incoherent.
3. Not in exclusive fullscreen. (You're never in exclusive fullscreen. Borderless is the modern answer and it's a 4.9 config concern.)

And you must **ask first** — `IDXGIFactory5::CheckFeatureSupport(DXGI_FEATURE_PRESENT_ALLOW_TEARING)`. It's a Windows 10+ / WDDM 2.0 capability, and on a machine without it, setting the flag fails swap-chain creation outright.

### The red herring in your own constructor

Look at what you build the window with:

```csharp
var options = WindowOptions.Default with { API = GraphicsAPI.None, ... };
```

`WindowOptions.Default` sets **`VSync = true`**. It has been sitting in your options object since 1.5, and it does nothing. Silk.NET's `VSync` is implemented as a swap-interval call on a graphics context that Silk owns — and you passed `GraphicsAPI.None`, which is you telling Silk *"create a bare window, I'll do the graphics."* There is no context for it to configure. `WindowOptions.Default` also sets `FramesPerSecond = 0` and `UpdatesPerSecond = 0`, meaning Silk's own software frame limiter is off too.

So there are three throttles in play and only one of them is real:

| Throttle | Where | Currently |
|---|---|---|
| `window.VSync` | Silk, needs a Silk-owned context | `true`, and inert |
| `window.FramesPerSecond` | Silk, a `Thread.Sleep`-grade limiter | `0` = off |
| `Present(syncInterval, flags)` | DXGI | **this is the one that's running your frame rate** |

Presentation is yours now. That was the deal in 1.5.

## Step 1 — three new fields

Edit **`Firmament.Core/FirmamentWindow.cs`**. Drop these in with the rest:

```csharp
private bool allowTearingSupported;
private uint presentSyncInterval = 1;
private uint swapChainFlags;
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `presentSyncInterval = 1` | We are changing the default from `0` to `1` — VSync **on**. **This is the line that introduces a cap you have never had**, so expect your FPS number to fall by three orders of magnitude the first time you run Step 7. That's the right default for a game: a coherent image, and it stops you burning a whole GPU redrawing a solid-color clear 10,000 times a second so that 9,940 of them can be thrown away. You'll toggle it at runtime in Step 5. |
| `uint`, not `int` | `Present`'s signature is `Present(uint SyncInterval, uint Flags)`. Matching the type keeps the casts out of the call site. |
| `swapChainFlags` | 1.7 told you this argument would stop being zero, and here it is. The flag has to be identical in `SwapChainDesc1.Flags` **and** in every `ResizeBuffers` call. One field, used twice, so they cannot drift. |

## Step 2 — ask the driver whether tearing is allowed

New method:

```csharp
private bool IsTearingSupported(ComPtr<IDXGIFactory2> factory)
{
	if (factory.QueryInterface(out ComPtr<IDXGIFactory5> factory5) < 0)
	{
		return false;
	}

	var allowTearing = 0;
	var result = factory5.CheckFeatureSupport(Silk.NET.DXGI.Feature.PresentAllowTearing, ref allowTearing, sizeof(int));

	factory5.Dispose();

	return result >= 0 && allowTearing != 0;
}
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `QueryInterface<IDXGIFactory5>` | COM's version negotiation. `IDXGIFactory5` is DXGI 1.5; you created an `IDXGIFactory2` (DXGI 1.2). `QueryInterface` asks the object "do you also speak this newer interface?" It's the same object either way — a successful QI just bumps the reference count and hands you a second pointer. |
| `< 0` on the HRESULT | An HRESULT's sign bit **is** the failure bit. `>= 0` is any success code, `< 0` is any failure. `E_NOINTERFACE` here means pre-Windows-10 DXGI, which is a legitimate "no", not an exception-worthy event — hence a plain `return false` rather than `ThrowHResult`. |
| `Silk.NET.DXGI.Feature`, fully qualified | **Not optional.** This file imports both `Silk.NET.DXGI` and `Silk.NET.Direct3D11`, and *both* declare a type named `Feature`. Bare `Feature.PresentAllowTearing` is a compile error: `CS0104: 'Feature' is an ambiguous reference`. Qualify it. |
| `var allowTearing = 0` | `CheckFeatureSupport` is a generic out-parameter API: you hand it a buffer and its size, and it fills it in. For this feature the buffer is a 4-byte Win32 `BOOL` — an `int`, where 0 is false and non-zero is true. Not a C# `bool` (1 byte), which is why the size argument would lie if you used one. |
| `sizeof(int)` | The size in bytes of the buffer you just passed. DXGI validates it against what the feature expects and returns `E_INVALIDARG` on a mismatch — this is the one place a wrong number fails loudly instead of silently. |
| `factory5.Dispose()` | The QI incremented the refcount. Release your pointer. The `IDXGIFactory2` you were handed is unaffected and still yours to dispose at the end of `OnLoad`. |
| Checking **both** results | A supported call that writes `0` means "this machine says no." An unsupported call leaves the variable untouched. Test the HRESULT *and* the value. |

> On a modern Windows 11 box with a recent GPU this returns `1`. The check still earns its place: it costs one call at startup and it turns "swap-chain creation mysteriously fails on someone else's laptop" into a branch you already wrote.

## Step 3 — reorder `OnLoad` and set the flag

The flag goes into the swap-chain **descriptor**, so you have to know the answer before you build the descriptor — and right now you create the factory *after* it. Move the factory creation up.

In `OnLoad`, the block currently reads:

```csharp
var swapChainDesc = new SwapChainDesc1
{
	BufferCount = 2,
	Format = Format.FormatB8G8R8A8Unorm,
	BufferUsage = DXGI.UsageRenderTargetOutput,
	SwapEffect = SwapEffect.FlipDiscard,
	SampleDesc = new SampleDesc(1, 0),
};

SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(dxgi.CreateDXGIFactory2(0, out ComPtr<IDXGIFactory2> factory));
```

Swap the order and add two lines:

```csharp
SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(dxgi.CreateDXGIFactory2(0, out ComPtr<IDXGIFactory2> factory));

allowTearingSupported = IsTearingSupported(factory);
swapChainFlags = allowTearingSupported ? (uint)SwapChainFlag.AllowTearing : 0;

var swapChainDesc = new SwapChainDesc1
{
	BufferCount = 2,
	Format = Format.FormatB8G8R8A8Unorm,
	BufferUsage = DXGI.UsageRenderTargetOutput,
	SwapEffect = SwapEffect.FlipDiscard,
	SampleDesc = new SampleDesc(1, 0),
	Flags = swapChainFlags,
};
```

Everything below (`CreateSwapChainForHwnd`, `factory.Dispose()`) stays exactly as it is.

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `SwapChainFlag.AllowTearing` | Value `2048` (`0x800`). Silk exposes the enum both as `AllowTearing` and `SwapChainFlagAllowTearing` — same value, pick the short one. |
| `(uint)` cast | `SwapChainDesc1.Flags` is a raw `uint` bitfield, not the enum type — DXGI flag fields are OR-able bitmasks and C# enums don't model that well without `[Flags]` ceremony. Cast at the boundary. |
| Set the flag even though you default to VSync **on** | The flag is a *capability* of the swap chain, fixed at creation. The per-frame decision is the `Present` flag. Declaring the capability costs nothing when unused and is the only way Step 5's toggle can work at runtime — you cannot add it later without destroying the swap chain. |
| `factory.Dispose()` unmoved | Still after `CreateSwapChainForHwnd`. Don't release the factory before you've used it. |

## Step 4 — present with the right flags

Add the flag chooser:

```csharp
private uint GetPresentFlags()
{
	if (presentSyncInterval == 0 && allowTearingSupported)
	{
		return DXGI.PresentAllowTearing;
	}

	return 0;
}
```

And change the present call in `OnRender`:

```csharp
// was: swapChain.Present(0, 0);
SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(swapChain.Present(presentSyncInterval, GetPresentFlags()));
```

Then update `ResizeSwapChain` so the flags match what you created the chain with:

```csharp
// was: ...ResizeBuffers(0, width, height, Format.FormatUnknown, 0));
SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(swapChain.ResizeBuffers(0, width, height, Format.FormatUnknown, swapChainFlags));
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `presentSyncInterval == 0 &&` | Rule 2 from the concept section. Sync interval `1` *plus* the tearing flag is `DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL`, immediately. The guard is not defensive coding, it's the API contract. |
| `&& allowTearingSupported` | Rule 1. Passing the present flag on a swap chain that wasn't created with the swap-chain flag is the same error. Both bits are driven off the one bool, so they can't disagree. |
| `DXGI.PresentAllowTearing` | Value `512` (`0x200`). Note it is **not** the same number as the swap-chain flag (`2048`) — two different bitfields that happen to both be about tearing. Don't reuse one for the other. |
| `SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult` on `Present` | New, and worth it: `Present` has been silently returning an HRESULT you've been discarding since 1.5. Every tearing misuse above reports itself here and nowhere else. `ThrowHResult` only throws on a negative HRESULT, so the *success* codes `Present` legitimately returns — notably `DXGI_STATUS_OCCLUDED` (`0x087A0001`, "your window is fully hidden") — pass through untouched. |
| `swapChainFlags` in `ResizeBuffers` | The promise 1.7 made. `ResizeBuffers` re-creates the buffers and must reproduce the creation flags exactly; a mismatch is `DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL` on the first window drag. This is why it's a field and not a literal in two places. |

## Step 5 — a key to cycle it

You cannot feel the difference between these modes by reading about them. Bind it.

```csharp
private void CyclePresentSyncInterval()
{
	presentSyncInterval = presentSyncInterval switch
	{
		0 => 1,
		1 => 2,
		_ => 0,
	};
}
```

Wire it into `OnKeyDown` alongside the two you already have:

```csharp
private void OnKeyDown(IKeyboard source, Key key, int scancode)
{
	RunActionForKeyDown(key, Key.Space, () => pauseBackgroundSwitch = !pauseBackgroundSwitch);
	RunActionForKeyDown(key, Key.Escape, () => window.Close());
	RunActionForKeyDown(key, Key.V, CyclePresentSyncInterval);
}
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `1 → 2 → 0` cycle | Half-rate is the interesting middle case: it proves the cap is the *display's* clock and not your code, because 30 is exactly half of 60 and nothing in your engine knows what 60 is. |
| `_ => 0` with no throw | The house rule about a throwing discard arm is for switching on an **enum or type**, where an unhandled case is a real bug. This is a `uint` cycle where every other value wrapping to `0` is the intended behavior, not an error. |
| `CyclePresentSyncInterval` passed as a method group | `RunActionForKeyDown` takes an `Action`; no lambda needed when the signature already matches. |
| No `Present` change needed | `GetPresentFlags()` reads the field every frame, so the flag follows the toggle with no extra plumbing. That's the payoff for computing it per-frame instead of caching it. |

## Step 6 — report it

Add the mode to the title bar. New method:

```csharp
private string DescribePresentMode()
{
	if (presentSyncInterval > 0)
	{
		return $"vsync {presentSyncInterval}";
	}

	if (allowTearingSupported)
	{
		return "tearing";
	}

	return "no-sync";
}
```

And in `ReportPerformance`, add it to the interpolated string:

```csharp
window.Title =
	$"Firmament - {framesPerSecond:F0} FPS | {avgMilliseconds:F2} ms avg | {peakMilliseconds:F2} ms peak | {updatesSinceLastReport} updates | {DescribePresentMode()} | Background Paused {pauseBackgroundSwitch} | {resizeCount} resizes | {backBufferWidth}x{backBufferHeight} @ {aspectRatio:F2}:1";
```

`"no-sync"` rather than `"tearing"` when the flag isn't available, and neither of them is called `"uncapped"`, on purpose. Both modes are uncapped in FPS — that's `FlipDiscard`, not the tearing flag. What actually differs is whether a frame can reach the scanout between refreshes, and `no-sync` says the honest thing: not synced to vblank, not tearing either, just handing DWM frames faster than it can look at them.

## ✅ Checkpoint

Note two numbers before you start: your monitor's refresh rate — `Settings → System → Display → Advanced display`, call it **R** — and the FPS your *current* build reports, which should be in the thousands. (On this machine the primary panel reports 59 Hz and a second one runs at 120 — if you drag the window between monitors, the numbers below follow the monitor, which is itself worth seeing.)

- **The first thing you'll notice is the FPS collapsing**, from five figures to R. That is Step 1 doing its job, not something breaking. You did not lose any *displayed* frames — you were only ever seeing R of them — you stopped rendering the ones nobody saw.
- Title bar reads `... | vsync 1 | ...` and **FPS ≈ R**, with **avg ms ≈ 1000/R** (≈16.7 ms at 60 Hz). Peak ms is close to avg — a solid-color clear has nothing to spike on.
- **Press V once** → `vsync 2`, and **FPS ≈ R/2**, avg ms ≈ doubled. Exactly half. This is the single most convincing demonstration in the lesson: nothing in your code knows the number 60, and yet it halved precisely.
- **Press V again** → `tearing` (or `no-sync`), and FPS jumps back to roughly the number you wrote down before you started — thousands, avg ms well under 1. That's `FlipDiscard` throwing frames away again. The display is still showing R of them; only the render rate moved.
- **Press V again** → back to `vsync 1` and FPS settles to R.
- **The color cycle runs at the same speed in all three modes.** This is the thing to actually stare at. A full transition still takes 5 seconds whether you're rendering 60 or 10,000 frames per second, because `AdvanceColorTransition(delta)` scales by time and not by frames. That's 1.2's lesson cashing a cheque, and it's what **3.11** will make a rule.
- Resizing still works — no `DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL` on the first drag. That's `swapChainFlags` being used in both places.
- ESC / gamepad Start still quit, SPACE / gamepad A still pause. Nothing regressed.

**On actually seeing a tear:** you probably won't, and that's expected. In `tearing` mode DXGI can only genuinely bypass composition when your swap chain gets "independent flip" — roughly, when the window covers the whole output. In a 1280×720 window on the desktop, DWM keeps compositing and you get the uncapped `Present` rate without a visible seam. Maximize, set the mode to `tearing`, and drag a high-contrast window across yours; if you see a horizontal discontinuity that moves vertically, that's it. A solid-color clear is close to the worst possible test image — there's no detail for a seam to interrupt. Revisit this at **2.6** when there's geometry moving.

## 🐛 Common failure modes

1. **`CS0104: 'Feature' is an ambiguous reference between 'Silk.NET.DXGI.Feature' and 'Silk.NET.Direct3D11.Feature'.`** Both namespaces are imported at the top of this file and both define `Feature`. Fully qualify it: `Silk.NET.DXGI.Feature.PresentAllowTearing`. (Don't "fix" it by dropping a using — you need both.)
2. **Exception on the very first frame, HRESULT `0x887A0001` (`DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL`), thrown from `Present`.** You're passing the tearing flag with a non-zero sync interval. Check the `presentSyncInterval == 0` half of `GetPresentFlags` — it's the guard people delete as redundant.
3. **Same HRESULT, but from `Present` only when `allowTearingSupported` is false.** You set the present flag without the swap-chain flag. Both come off the same bool for a reason; make sure `Flags = swapChainFlags` is actually in the descriptor and you didn't paste the descriptor without it.
4. **Same HRESULT, thrown from `ResizeBuffers` the first time you drag the window.** Creation flags and resize flags disagree — you added `Flags` to `SwapChainDesc1` but left the literal `0` in `ResizeBuffers`. Pass `swapChainFlags` in both.
5. **`CreateSwapChainForHwnd` fails at startup with `E_INVALIDARG` (`0x80070057`).** You set `SwapChainFlag.AllowTearing` unconditionally instead of gating it on `IsTearingSupported`. On hardware that doesn't support it, the flag is rejected at creation, not at present.
6. **Pressing V does nothing.** The keyboard handler is `KeyDown`, an event — check the new `RunActionForKeyDown` line is actually inside `OnKeyDown` and that `keyboard` wasn't null at load. Remember your `GetKeyboard` uses `FirstOrDefault`, so a machine that enumerates no keyboard silently skips the subscription.
7. **FPS stays at ~R in *every* mode, including `tearing` / `no-sync`.** This is *not* a tearing-support problem — `FlipDiscard` gives you an uncapped render rate at sync interval `0` whether or not the tearing flag was granted, which is exactly what you had before Step 1. So the sync interval isn't reaching `Present`: check that `OnRender` passes `presentSyncInterval` and not the literal you replaced, and that the title text is genuinely changing when you press V. (A title that says `tearing` while the FPS says R means the field is right and the call site is stale.)
8. **FPS is enormous but the color transition also races.** Then you have a real bug, and it's not in this lesson — something in `AdvanceColorTransition` stopped using `delta`. Frame rate must not change simulation speed.
9. **`vsync 1` gives you exactly half your refresh rate.** You're missing the vblank deadline every frame. On a clear-only frame that means something else is stalling the thread — a debugger attached, a breakpoint, or an unusually chatty overlay. It'll be back.

## 🔧 Exercises

1. **Prove `window.VSync` is inert.** Add `VSync = false` to the `WindowOptions` initializer. Run. Nothing changes — you stay pinned to your refresh rate in `vsync 1` mode. Now set `presentSyncInterval` to `0` and watch it fly. Two minutes to permanently internalize which layer owns presentation. Put the option back the way it was (or delete it — it's noise either way).
2. **Try Silk's frame limiter.** Set `FramesPerSecond = 30` in `WindowOptions` while in `tearing` mode. It works, and it's a *different mechanism* — a sleep in Silk's loop, not a hardware sync. Watch `peak ms` while you do it: sleep-based limiters are jittery in a way vsync isn't, and the peak number will show it where the average hides it. Then take it back out. **4.2** is where frame pacing gets done properly.
3. **Triple-buffer it.** Change `BufferCount` to `3` and compare `vsync 1` against the 2-buffer version. FPS is unchanged (the display's clock didn't move) but you've given yourself one more frame of slack before `Present` blocks — the classic latency-for-smoothness trade. Note in your notes which one you'd ship, and why "more buffers" is not free.
4. **Put it on the gamepad.** One line in `OnGamepadButtonDown` using your existing `RunActionForGamepadButtonDown` — `ButtonName.B` is unclaimed. Trivial, but it makes the point that `CyclePresentSyncInterval` is a *command* with two bindings, and commands-with-bindings is exactly the shape **4.7**'s input manager wants.
5. **Pin the uncapped rate on the right thing.** The concept section claims your five-figure FPS comes from `FlipDiscard` and *not* from the tearing flag. Test it: leave the mode on `tearing`, and temporarily set `SwapEffect = SwapEffect.FlipSequential` in the descriptor (the tearing flag is still legal there, so nothing else has to change). `FlipSequential` preserves buffer contents and queues presents instead of replacing them, so with `BufferCount = 2` the frame rate should fall back toward R. Whatever you measure, write the number down — this is the one experiment that separates "the flip effect uncapped me" from "the tearing flag uncapped me," and they are constantly conflated online. Put `FlipDiscard` back; it's the one you want.
6. **Make the peak honest.** In `tearing` mode at 1000+ FPS, `peak ms` is measuring noise. Add a running count of how many frames in the last report interval exceeded 1.5× the vsync deadline (`1000.0 / R` ms) and print it. That's a **frame time spike counter**, and it is the single most useful number in the title bar — it catches hitches an average will never show you. **11.1** builds this out for real.
