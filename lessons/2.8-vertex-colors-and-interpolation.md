# Lesson 2.8 — Vertex Colors and How the Rasterizer Interpolates

> Part 2 — The Pipeline and the First Triangle
> *Vertex colors and how the rasterizer interpolates between vertices*

**Where you already are:** the triangle draws, and since 2.7 the plumbing is in one place. `Shader` (in `Firmament.Core/Shaders/`) owns the compile, the two shader objects and the input layout, and releases them once. `TriangleDrawer` owns the immutable vertex buffer, the `InputElementDesc[]`, the topology and the `Draw(3, 0)`. `pass-through.hlsl` is an embedded resource read through `IShaderLoader`.

This lesson adds no new D3D11 object, no new C# class, and nothing to the vertex buffer. It is a lesson about the one pipeline stage you cannot call an API on: the **rasterizer**, and specifically its interpolator — the fixed-function hardware that takes the three colors you uploaded and turns them into one color for each of the ~180,000 pixels your triangle covers at 1280×720. You will spend the lesson editing five lines of HLSL, running, and looking. One line stays at the end.

## Divergences from the curriculum — read first

- **"Vertex colors" is already done.** The curriculum line for 2.8 reads *"Vertex colors and how the rasterizer interpolates between vertices"*, written back when 2.6 was expected to end on a flat-colored triangle. It did not — `Vertex` has carried `R`, `G`, `B` since 2.2, the `COLOR` element has sat at `AlignedByteOffset = 8` since 2.3, and `pass-through.hlsl` has passed it through since 2.4. There is nothing to add. **This lesson is the second half of that line only**, and it is the more interesting half.
- **You have no MSAA.** `SwapChainDesc1.SampleDesc = new SampleDesc(1, 0)` — one sample per pixel. Two of HLSL's five interpolation modifiers (`centroid`, `sample`) exist purely to control *where inside a multisampled pixel* an attribute is evaluated, so today they compile and do exactly nothing. They are covered below anyway, in two sentences, because the day you turn on MSAA is the day you need to already know they exist.
- **Your back buffer is `FormatB8G8R8A8Unorm`.** Eight bits per channel, and `UNORM` means the value you return from the pixel shader is **clamped to `[0, 1]`** and then quantized to 256 steps. Return `1.7` and you get `1.0`. That clamp is invisible today and becomes load-bearing in 7.3, where a bright-pass filter needs values *above* 1 to survive — which is what an HDR float render target is for.
- **You are on `ps_5_0`, so `SV_Barycentrics` does not exist.** Shader Model 6.1 hands the pixel shader the rasterizer's barycentric weights directly as a system value. On SM5 you cannot ask for them — but as Step 1 shows, you are already smuggling them in by hand and did not know it.
- **`Vertex` uses auto-properties under `[StructLayout(LayoutKind.Sequential)]`.** That is a real, if quiet, problem, and this is the lesson that touches that struct. See the optional Step 6 — it is four lines and I would take it.

## Concept: what actually happens between your vertex shader and your pixel shader

Your vertex shader runs **three times**. Your pixel shader runs roughly **180,000 times**. Nothing you wrote bridges that gap. The rasterizer does, and it is the only major stage in D3D11 with no object to create and no `RSSet*` call for the part that matters — it is hardwired. Understanding it is the difference between "the colors blend, I guess" and being able to predict what a shader will do before you run it.

Three things happen, in order.

**1. Triangle setup.** After the vertex shader, clip-space positions are divided by `w` (the perspective divide) and mapped through your viewport into pixel coordinates. The rasterizer takes those three 2D points and builds three **edge functions** — one per edge. An edge function is a linear function of `(x, y)` that is zero *on* the edge, positive on one side and negative on the other. Geometrically it is the 2D cross product of "the edge vector" and "the vector from the edge's start to the pixel", which is twice the signed area of the triangle those three points form. You will build this same tool by hand in 3.3, and again in 6.6 for point-in-polygon collision. Same math; the hardware just does it billions of times a second.

**2. Coverage.** A pixel is inside the triangle when all three edge functions agree in sign. Pixels lying exactly *on* an edge are decided by the **top-left fill rule**: a pixel on a shared edge is claimed by exactly one of the two triangles, never both and never neither. That is not pedantry — it is why the two triangles of 2.9's quad will not show a seam or a double-blended line down the diagonal.

**3. Interpolation.** This is the part you are here for.

### Barycentric coordinates, geometrically

Pick any point **P** inside a triangle with corners v0, v1, v2. Draw lines from P to all three corners. You have cut the triangle into three smaller ones. Now ask: *what fraction of the total area is the sub-triangle opposite v0?* Call it λ0. Same for λ1 and λ2.

Those three fractions are the **barycentric coordinates** of P. Everything you need about them:

- They always sum to **1** — the three pieces are the whole triangle.
- Inside the triangle they are all **≥ 0**. Outside, at least one goes negative. That *is* the coverage test from step 2, which is why setup and interpolation share hardware.
- At a corner they are a basis vector: at v0, λ = (1, 0, 0). At the midpoint of edge v0–v1, λ = (0.5, 0.5, 0). At the centroid, (⅓, ⅓, ⅓).
- λ0 is "how much of v0 is in this point" — a weight. Close to v0, λ0 is near 1.

Interpolating an attribute is then a weighted average: `value(P) = λ0·value(v0) + λ1·value(v1) + λ2·value(v2)`. Every varying attribute rides those same three numbers — color today, texture coordinates in 5.4, normals in 8.8. The hardware does not recompute area ratios per pixel; because that expression is *linear in x and y*, setup fits each attribute a **plane equation** and evaluates it per pixel with a couple of adds. That is why twelve more floats of per-vertex data cost almost nothing across a full-screen triangle. It is also, exactly, why the rasterizer can only interpolate **linearly**: a plane is all it has.

### The `w` divide, and why it is invisible today

Straight lines stay straight under perspective, but **even spacing does not stay even**. Think of railroad ties: evenly spaced in the world, bunched toward the horizon on screen. If the rasterizer averaged attributes using screen-space weights, a texture on a floor receding into the distance would smear — the classic PlayStation 1 wobble, which is precisely a console that skipped this step.

The fix: interpolate `attribute / w` and `1 / w` linearly in screen space, then divide the first by the second per pixel. That is **perspective-correct interpolation**, and it is the default (`linear`) in HLSL.

Today your vertex shader writes `float4(input.position, 0.0f, 1.0f)` — `w` is the constant `1.0` for every vertex. `1/w` is 1 everywhere, so the correction divides by one, and perspective-correct and plain screen-space interpolation are **bit-identical**. You will not be able to tell them apart in Step 4, and that is the point: you get the mechanism now, in the case where it cannot bite you, so that in 8.1 — when `w` becomes view-space depth and the divide starts doing real work — it is already familiar.

### Where the modifiers live

HLSL's interpolation modifiers go on struct members, and this trips people: **only the modifier on the *pixel shader's input* matters.** The vertex shader's output declaration does not control interpolation — interpolation happens after the VS is done. You share one `vertex_output` struct between the two stages (2.4), which is a good habit for keeping the signatures honest, and it means one edit changes the thing that counts.

The five modifiers, complete:

| Modifier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `linear` | The default. Perspective-correct linear interpolation. |
| `noperspective` | Interpolate in screen space; skip the `1/w` divide. |
| `nointerpolation` | Do not interpolate at all — take the value from the **provoking vertex** (flat shading). |
| `centroid` | Evaluate at a sample point *guaranteed inside* the triangle rather than at the pixel center. MSAA only; a no-op for you today. |
| `sample` | Evaluate per sample instead of per pixel, forcing the PS to run per sample. MSAA only. |

One hard rule that will bite you eventually: an **integer** attribute (`uint`, `int`) *must* be `nointerpolation`. There is no sensible linear blend between two integers, and the compiler refuses rather than guessing. Exercise 4.

### The provoking vertex

With `nointerpolation`, the whole triangle takes the attribute from one chosen vertex. D3D11's convention is the **first** vertex of the primitive — the *leading* vertex. (OpenGL's default is the *last*; if you ever port flat-shaded code between the two, this is the bug.) D3D11 gives you no switch for it. For your `Draw(3, 0)` on a triangle list, the provoking vertex is your buffer's element 0 — the red one at the top.

### Pixels come in 2×2 quads

One last fact worth carrying: the pixel shader never runs on a lone pixel. Hardware always shades a **2×2 block**, even when only one pixel of it is covered — the other three run as *helper pixels* whose output is discarded. The reason is `ddx`/`ddy`: a shader can ask "how much did this value change moving one pixel right?", and the only cheap way to answer is to have the neighbour's value sitting in the next lane. 5.4's mipmap selection is built on exactly this. The cost is that a triangle covering one pixel still costs four shader invocations, which is why ten thousand tiny triangles are disproportionately expensive — a 9.8 (LOD) problem, noted here so it is not a surprise later.

## Reading the triangle you already have

```csharp
Vertex[] vertices = [new(0.0f, 0.5f, 1f, 0f, 0f), new(0.5f, -0.5f, 0f, 1f, 0f), new(-0.5f, -0.5f, 0f, 0f, 1f)];
```

Vertex 0 is pure red `(1,0,0)`, vertex 1 pure green `(0,1,0)`, vertex 2 pure blue `(0,0,1)`. Those are the three **basis vectors**. So for any pixel:

```
color = λ0·(1,0,0) + λ1·(0,1,0) + λ2·(0,0,1) = (λ0, λ1, λ2)
```

The color your pixel shader receives *is* the barycentric coordinate, channel for channel. Red is "how much vertex 0". Your triangle has been a barycentric debug visualizer this whole time. That is not a coincidence of this lesson — it is why an RGB-corner triangle is the universal graphics hello-world, and it is what makes the next five experiments cost one line each.

## Steps — what to write and where

Every step edits **`Firmament.Core/Shaders/pass-through.hlsl`** only. No C# changes until Step 6, which is optional. Build, run, look, move on — the file ends the lesson one line different from how it started.

> Your shader is an **embedded resource** (2.7's note): it is compiled into `Firmament.Core.dll` at build time, so every edit needs a rebuild, not just a restart. The disk-watching version arrives in 4.4.

### Step 0 — The one line that stays

In `vertex_output`, make the default explicit:

```diff
 struct vertex_output
 {
 	float4 position : SV_POSITION;
-	float3 color : COLOR;
+	linear float3 color : COLOR;
 };
```

`linear` is what you already had — this changes no behavior at all. It is there because for the rest of this lesson you will be swapping that keyword, and because "which interpolation mode is this attribute using?" should be answerable by reading the struct rather than by remembering a default. Leave it in when you are done.

### Step 1 — Prove the weights sum to 1

Replace the pixel shader body:

```hlsl
float4 pixel_main(vertex_output input) : SV_TARGET
{
	float weightSum = input.color.r + input.color.g + input.color.b;

	return float4(weightSum, weightSum, weightSum, 1.0f);
}
```

**Expect:** a uniformly **white** triangle. Not a gradient — flat, even white, edge to edge.

That is the λ0 + λ1 + λ2 = 1 identity, verified by your GPU on every pixel. If any pixel were even slightly off you would see a smudge of grey. It is also the cheapest possible proof that the rasterizer is genuinely computing weights per pixel rather than doing something approximate.

### Step 2 — Make the weights visible as numbers

```hlsl
float4 pixel_main(vertex_output input) : SV_TARGET
{
	float highestWeight = max(input.color.r, max(input.color.g, input.color.b));

	return float4(step(highestWeight, input.color), 1.0f);
}
```

`step(edge, x)` returns 1 where `x >= edge` and 0 otherwise, componentwise — so the winning channel becomes 1 and the losers 0. Each pixel is painted the pure color of *whichever vertex it is nearest to in barycentric terms*.

**Expect:** the smooth gradient collapses into **three hard-edged regions** — red, green and blue — meeting at a single point in the middle.

The geometry is worth a pause. The boundary between the red and green regions is the set of pixels where λ0 = λ1, which is the line through vertex 2 and the midpoint of the opposite edge — a **median**. All three medians meet at the **centroid**, where λ = (⅓, ⅓, ⅓), and that is the meeting point you are looking at. You just rendered a Voronoi diagram of three points with no branching and no distance calculations, because the rasterizer had already done the work. That is the mental move worth keeping from this lesson: an interpolated attribute is *data you can compute with*, not just a color to output.

### Step 3 — Turn interpolation off

Restore the original body:

```hlsl
float4 pixel_main(vertex_output input) : SV_TARGET
{
	return float4(input.color, 1.0f);
}
```

and change the modifier in `vertex_output`:

```diff
-	linear float3 color : COLOR;
+	nointerpolation float3 color : COLOR;
```

**Expect:** a solid **red** triangle. No gradient anywhere.

Red because D3D11's provoking vertex is the **first** vertex of the primitive, and your element 0 is the red one. Prove that rather than believing it — in `TriangleDrawer.CreateVertexBuffer`, **rotate** the array so green leads:

```csharp
Vertex[] vertices = [new(0.5f, -0.5f, 0f, 1f, 0f), new(-0.5f, -0.5f, 0f, 0f, 1f), new(0.0f, 0.5f, 1f, 0f, 0f)];
```

The triangle turns solid **green** and covers exactly the same pixels. Rotate — do not just swap two entries — because a swap reverses the winding order, and clockwise versus counter-clockwise is something D3D11 has opinions about the moment backface culling matters (8.7). Right now `RSSetState` is never called, so you get the default rasterizer state, which culls back faces and treats clockwise as front. A 2D triangle that vanishes when you reorder its vertices is that default talking.

Put the vertex order back the way it was.

### Step 4 — The non-experiment

```diff
-	nointerpolation float3 color : COLOR;
+	noperspective float3 color : COLOR;
```

**Expect:** the original smooth RGB triangle, pixel for pixel identical to `linear`. Screenshot each and diff them if you like — they will match exactly.

This is the one result in the lesson you should be *unable* to see, and it is worth running deliberately to feel the size of the thing you are not seeing. Your vertex shader hard-codes `w = 1.0`, so the `1/w` correction is a division by one. In 8.1 that `w` becomes view-space depth, this same one-word change starts warping every texture on every surface not parallel to the screen, and `noperspective` turns into something you reach for on purpose — for screen-space effects and 2D overlays where raw screen-space interpolation is what you actually want.

### Step 5 — Contour the gradient (optional, 30 seconds)

```hlsl
float4 pixel_main(vertex_output input) : SV_TARGET
{
	return float4(floor(input.color * 8.0f) / 8.0f, 1.0f);
}
```

`8.0f` is an arbitrary band count — pick anything; 8 gives visibly chunky bands without turning to mush. `floor(x * n) / n` snaps each channel down to the nearest rung of an n-step ladder.

**Expect:** the gradient breaks into a stack of flat bands with visible boundaries — a contour map of the barycentric field. The bands are straight and evenly spaced, which is the plane-equation claim from the Concept section shown directly: contour a linear function and you get parallel straight lines. If interpolation were anything but linear, these contours would curve.

### Step 6 — Optional but recommended: fix the `Vertex` layout contract

Nothing to do with the rasterizer; everything to do with this lesson being the one that touches that struct. `Firmament.Core/Types/Vertex.cs` is declared `[StructLayout(LayoutKind.Sequential, Pack = 4)]` — and then declares its five floats as **auto-properties**.

`LayoutKind.Sequential` promises that *fields* are laid out in declaration order. Auto-properties are not fields; they are a pair of methods over a compiler-generated backing field named `<X>k__BackingField`, and **the ordering of compiler-generated backing fields is explicitly not guaranteed by the CLR**. Roslyn emits them in declaration order today and this works. Nothing contractually stops that from changing, and the failure mode is not a compile error — it is `AlignedByteOffset = 8` silently pointing at `Y`, and a triangle with wrong colors or wrong positions that you would chase through RenderDoc for an hour in 11.3.

Anything whose bytes are copied verbatim to the GPU should be plain fields:

```csharp
using System.Runtime.InteropServices;

namespace Firmament.Core.Types;

[StructLayout(LayoutKind.Sequential, Pack = 4)]
public struct Vertex(float x, float y, float r, float g, float b)
{
	public float X = x;

	public float Y = y;

	public float R = r;

	public float G = g;

	public float B = b;
}
```

`.editorconfig` prefers auto-properties at **suggestion** severity, not warning, so this will not fight `dotnet format` — and that preference is about API design, which is not what this type is. It is a byte layout with names on it. No call site changes: `new(0.0f, 0.5f, 1f, 0f, 0f)` and `sizeof(Vertex)` both behave exactly as before.

### Finish

Put `pass-through.hlsl` back to the pass-through body with `linear` on the color, and build clean.

```hlsl
struct vertex_output
{
	float4 position : SV_POSITION;
	linear float3 color : COLOR;
};
```

## Per-value justification

| Value / call | Why |
|---|---|
| `linear` (kept) | The HLSL default, written out. Perspective-correct linear interpolation, `1/w` divide included. Explicit because this attribute's interpolation mode is now something you know you can change. |
| `nointerpolation` | Flat shading: the attribute is copied from the provoking vertex with no blending. Required — not optional — on any integer varying. |
| `noperspective` | Screen-space interpolation, skipping the `1/w` divide. Identical to `linear` while `w == 1.0`; the correct choice for genuinely screen-space data once `w` varies. |
| `centroid` / `sample` | Control *where within a multisampled pixel* the attribute is evaluated. `SampleDesc(1, 0)` means one sample, so both are no-ops today. Listed so the words are not new when MSAA is. |
| `step(highestWeight, input.color)` | `step(edge, x)` is `x >= edge ? 1 : 0`, componentwise. One instruction, no branch — a branch in a pixel shader costs both sides across the whole 2×2 quad. |
| `8.0f` in `floor(color * 8.0f) / 8.0f` | Band count, chosen for visibility, not meaning. Any n works; the point is that quantizing a linear field yields evenly spaced straight contours. |
| Rotating rather than swapping the vertex array | A rotation preserves winding order; a swap reverses it. The default rasterizer state culls back faces with clockwise as front, so a swap can make the triangle disappear and send you chasing the wrong bug (8.7). |
| Fields instead of auto-properties on `Vertex` | `LayoutKind.Sequential` orders fields. The ordering of auto-property backing fields is not guaranteed by the CLR, and this struct's bytes go straight into a GPU buffer whose offsets are hard-coded in `DescribeVertexLayout`. |
| `SV_POSITION` in the pixel shader (Exercise 1) | The same semantic means different things per stage: the VS *writes* clip space; the PS *reads* pixel coordinates — `xy` the pixel center, `z` depth in `[0,1]`, `w` the reciprocal `1/w`. |

## 🐛 Common failure modes

1. **You edit the `.hlsl` and nothing changes.** It is an `EmbeddedResource`; the text is baked into `Firmament.Core.dll` at build. Rebuild, do not just re-run. If you rebuilt and it *still* has not changed, check you edited `Firmament.Core/Shaders/pass-through.hlsl` and not a copy under `bin/`.
2. **Step 1 gives a grey or gradient triangle instead of white.** You summed two channels instead of three, or built the `float4` from the `float3` plus the sum. Compare against the snippet — the sum is a single `float` broadcast to all three channels.
3. **Solid black triangle after an edit.** The pixel shader is returning zero. Usual cause: `step` arguments in the wrong order (`step(input.color, highestWeight)` compiles and means something else entirely), or a `float3`/`float4` mismatch that HLSL silently truncated rather than rejected.
4. **The triangle vanished after Step 3's reorder.** You swapped two vertices instead of rotating all three, reversed the winding, and the default rasterizer state culled it. Rotate `[a,b,c]` → `[b,c,a]`.
5. **A compile exception naming `vertex_main` or `pixel_main`.** Good — 2.7's error message doing its job. It carries the compiler's line number; read it. The likeliest cause here is an interpolation modifier on the wrong line or a stray `float3`/`float4`.
6. **Wrong colors in the corners after Step 6.** If you converted `Vertex` to fields and the colors are now scrambled, you reordered the members while editing. Declaration order *is* the byte layout, and `DescribeVertexLayout` hard-codes `AlignedByteOffset = 8` for `COLOR`. X, Y, R, G, B — in that order.

## 🔧 Exercises

1. **Color by screen position instead of by vertex.** Ignore `input.color` and return `float4(input.position.x / 1280.0f, input.position.y / 720.0f, 0.0f, 1.0f)`. You get a red-green gradient locked to the *window*, not to the triangle — drag the window around and it does not move; the triangle is a viewport onto it. Then resize the window and watch it go wrong, because `1280` and `720` are hard-coded lies. That is precisely the problem 2.11's constant buffer solves: getting a number from C# into a shader, per frame.
2. **Ask the hardware for the gradient.** Return `float4(abs(ddx(input.color)) * 200.0f, 1.0f)`. `ddx` is the difference between this pixel's value and its horizontal neighbour's inside the 2×2 quad — a per-pixel derivative, on the order of a thousandth of a channel here, hence the `200.0f` purely to make it visible. **Expect a perfectly flat color across the whole triangle.** That flatness proves the Concept section's claim from the other direction: the derivative of a plane is a constant. Try `ddy` too. This is the machinery 5.4 uses to pick a mip level.
3. **Watch the rasterizer draw.** Create an `ID3D11RasterizerState` with `FillMode = FillMode.Wireframe` and `CullMode = CullMode.None`, bind it with `RSSetState` before the draw, and toggle it on a key. You will be looking at the three edges whose edge functions decide every coverage test in this lesson. It is one more COM object to dispose — put it in `TriangleDrawer` beside the vertex buffer, release it in `Dispose`, and be honest that it is 8.7's material arriving six parts early because it is *useful*, not because it is in scope.
4. **Break it on purpose, twice.** (a) Add `uint id : TEXCOORD0;` to `vertex_output` without `nointerpolation` and read the compiler's refusal — then add the modifier and watch it compile. (b) Split `vertex_output` into two identical structs, one for the VS output and one for the PS input, and put `nointerpolation` on the **VS output** only. It compiles, and the triangle stays smooth: the VS declaration is ignored, only the PS input's modifier drives interpolation. Then move the modifier to the PS input struct alone and get flat shading. That asymmetry is worth having proven yourself once.

## 📎 What this unlocks

2.9 replaces the three-vertex triangle with a four-vertex quad drawn as two triangles through an index buffer and `DrawIndexed` — and every claim in this lesson gets a second, sharper test. The two triangles share an edge, so the top-left fill rule is what stops the diagonal from being double-shaded or missed. They share two *vertices*, so the shared corners feed both triangles literally the same interpolated inputs, which is the only reason a quad made of two independently rasterized triangles looks like one continuous surface at all.
