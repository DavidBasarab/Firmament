# Lesson 1.7 — Window Resize, the Viewport, and Aspect Ratio

> Part 1 — A Window and a Heartbeat
> *Window resize, viewport, aspect ratio, and what happens if you ignore it*

**Where you already are:** your committed `FirmamentWindow` opens a 1280×720 D3D11 host, builds a device / swap chain / render-target view once in `OnLoad`, cycles a clear color through ten `System.Drawing.Color`s, reports FPS + avg ms + peak ms + update count in the title bar, and listens to a keyboard *and* a gamepad. Everything in it assumes one thing that has been quietly true and is about to stop being true: **the back buffer is 1280×720 and always will be.** Drag the window edge and that assumption breaks. This lesson fixes it, and introduces the viewport — the thing that decides which pixels of that buffer the rasterizer is allowed to touch.

**Divergences from the 1.6 doc, since you tweaked it (as expected):**

- You used `input.Keyboards.FirstOrDefault()` / `input.Gamepads.FirstOrDefault()` instead of `[0]`. Fine — and it dodges the `IndexOutOfRangeException` failure mode 1.6 warned about, at the cost of a `NullReferenceException` on the very next line if nothing is plugged in. A gamepad-less machine will crash in `OnLoad` today. Worth a guard eventually; not this lesson.
- You took 1.6's exercise 2 and made SPACE an **event toggle** (`pauseBackgroundSwitch`) rather than the hold-to-freeze poll the doc handed out. So there is currently **no `IsKeyPressed` poll anywhere in the codebase** — the level-input half of 1.6 exists only in your head. It comes back for real when there's a ship to thrust.
- You wired the gamepad (`Start` closes, `A` toggles the pause) — that's 1.6's exercise 3 taken well past its stopping point, and it's a chunk of 13.7 pulled forward. No harm; just know it's ahead of the curriculum.
- `RunActionForKeyDown` / `RunActionForGamepadButtonDown` are your own invention. They read fine now. When 4.7's input manager lands, that pair is the first thing it should absorb.

## Concept: two rectangles that are only accidentally the same size

There are **two independent rectangles** in play, and today they are equal only because nobody has touched the window yet.

**The window** is an OS object. Its client area is measured in pixels and the user changes it whenever they feel like it.

**The back buffer** is a GPU texture. It was allocated exactly once, in `OnLoad`, at a fixed size. Look at your `SwapChainDesc1` — you never set `Width` or `Height`, so they defaulted to `0`, and `0` is DXGI's way of saying *"go ask the HWND how big it is."* It asked once. It will never ask again.

So what happens when you drag the border? Not a crash. DXGI's default `Scaling` mode is *stretch*: it keeps presenting your stale 1280×720 texture and lets the compositor smear it across the new client area. Grow the window and you get a soft, upscaled, wrong-resolution image. Shrink it and you throw away pixels you paid to render. It looks *almost* right, which is exactly why this bug survives to ship.

The fix is `ResizeBuffers` — throw the old textures away, allocate new ones at the new size. The catch, and the whole reason this lesson has failure modes: **DXGI cannot free a buffer that anyone still holds a reference to.** You hold one (your `renderTargetView` points at back buffer 0). The device context holds a *second, invisible* one, because you bound that view to the output-merger stage in `OnRender` and D3D11 keeps its own reference to whatever is bound. Miss either and `ResizeBuffers` fails.

### The viewport, geometrically

The rasterizer does not think in pixels. It thinks in **normalized device coordinates** — a fixed box running −1 to +1 in x and −1 to +1 in y, *no matter what shape your window is*. That box is where geometry lives after the vertex shader is done with it.

The **viewport** is the map from that box onto a pixel rectangle of the back buffer. −1 in x lands on the rectangle's left edge, +1 on its right edge, and the same for y. Nothing more.

Here is the consequence, and it's the entire aspect-ratio problem: NDC is *square* and your window is *not*. Map a square onto a 1280×720 rectangle and the x axis gets stretched by 1280 units-per-NDC while y gets 720. Draw a circle in NDC, get an ellipse on screen. Draw your ship, turn it 90°, and it changes shape as it rotates. That's not a bug in your art — it's the viewport doing exactly what you told it to.

**Aspect ratio** is just the number that quantifies the squash: `width / height`. 1280×720 → 1.78. You fix the distortion in one of two places: squeeze the viewport down to a sub-rectangle of the right shape (letterboxing — exercise 3), or bake the correction into a projection matrix (**3.6**, orthographic projection, and that's the real answer). Today we do neither. Today we just make the viewport *track the buffer*, so it is correct and — more importantly — so it is not empty.

Two honest notes before you write anything:

1. **D3D11 has no default viewport.** The rasterizer starts with an empty one, width 0. Draw a triangle without ever calling `RSSetViewports` and you get a black screen with no error, no warning, nothing. That's a top-three cause of "my first triangle doesn't show up," and you're about to be immune to it.
2. **You will not see the viewport do anything this lesson.** `ClearRenderTargetView` ignores it completely — it clears the whole resource, viewport or no viewport. The viewport only gates *rasterization*, and you have no geometry until **2.6**. So today the viewport call is correctness you're banking, not a visual. The observable part of this lesson is the resize itself, reported in the title bar.

## Step 1 — subscribe to the resize event

Edit **`Firmament.Core/FirmamentWindow.cs`**. In the constructor, next to the other three subscriptions:

```csharp
window.Load += OnLoad;
window.Update += OnUpdate;
window.Render += OnRender;
window.FramebufferResize += OnFramebufferResize;   // new
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `FramebufferResize`, not `Resize` | Silk.NET raises **both**, and they are not the same measurement. `Resize` reports the window's client area in *screen coordinates*; `FramebufferResize` reports it in *pixels*. On Windows at 100% scaling they're identical, which is why the distinction looks academic — but a back buffer is allocated in pixels, so pixels is what you want. Use the one that's defined in the units you're about to pass to `ResizeBuffers`. |
| Signature | `Action<Vector2D<int>>` — Silk.NET hands you the new size directly, so you never have to go re-read `window.FramebufferSize` inside the handler. |
| Why the constructor | It's an event on an object that already exists. Unlike the D3D and input setup, it doesn't need a native handle, so it belongs with its three siblings. |

No new `using` — `Vector2D<T>` comes from `Silk.NET.Maths`, already imported.

## Step 2 — pull render-target creation out of `OnLoad`

You currently build the view inline at the bottom of `OnLoad`. Resize needs to do the identical thing, so lift it into a method. **Delete** these three statements from `OnLoad`:

```csharp
SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(swapChain.GetBuffer(0, out ComPtr<ID3D11Texture2D> backBuffer));

SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(device.CreateRenderTargetView(backBuffer, null, ref renderTargetView));

backBuffer.Dispose();
```

and replace them with two calls:

```csharp
CreateRenderTargetView();
SetViewport((uint)window.FramebufferSize.X, (uint)window.FramebufferSize.Y);
```

Then add the method itself (body is byte-for-byte what you deleted):

```csharp
private void CreateRenderTargetView()
{
	SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(swapChain.GetBuffer(0, out ComPtr<ID3D11Texture2D> backBuffer));

	SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(device.CreateRenderTargetView(backBuffer, null, ref renderTargetView));

	backBuffer.Dispose();
}
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `window.FramebufferSize` | The initial size, in pixels — the same quantity the event will later hand you. The swap chain already allocated itself at this size (that's what the `0` width/height meant), so we're not resizing here, only telling the rasterizer where the buffer is. |
| Set a viewport at load at all | Because D3D11's default is empty. Even though nothing rasterizes yet, leaving this out means 2.6 silently draws nothing and you spend an evening blaming your shader. |
| `backBuffer.Dispose()` still there | `GetBuffer` hands you a reference; the RTV holds its own. Release yours immediately — leaving it alive is exactly the reference that makes `ResizeBuffers` fail in Step 3. |

## Step 3 — the resize handler

Three new methods and three new fields. Fields first, next to the others:

```csharp
private uint backBufferWidth;
private uint backBufferHeight;
private int resizeCount;
```

The handler:

```csharp
private void OnFramebufferResize(Vector2D<int> size)
{
	if (size.X <= 0 || size.Y <= 0)
	{
		return;
	}

	resizeCount++;

	ResizeSwapChain((uint)size.X, (uint)size.Y);
}
```

The release-and-reallocate dance:

```csharp
private void ResizeSwapChain(uint width, uint height)
{
	var noRenderTargets = default(ComPtr<ID3D11RenderTargetView>);

	deviceContext.OMSetRenderTargets(0, ref noRenderTargets, (ComPtr<ID3D11DepthStencilView>)default);

	renderTargetView.Dispose();

	SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult(swapChain.ResizeBuffers(0, width, height, Format.FormatUnknown, 0));

	CreateRenderTargetView();
	SetViewport(width, height);
}
```

And the viewport:

```csharp
private void SetViewport(uint width, uint height)
{
	var viewport = new Viewport
	{
		TopLeftX = 0f,
		TopLeftY = 0f,
		Width = width,
		Height = height,
		MinDepth = 0f,
		MaxDepth = 1f,
	};

	deviceContext.RSSetViewports(1, in viewport);

	backBufferWidth = width;
	backBufferHeight = height;
}
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `size.X <= 0 \|\| size.Y <= 0` | **Minimizing the window fires this event with 0×0.** A zero-area swap chain is invalid; `ResizeBuffers` returns `E_INVALIDARG` and `ThrowHResult` turns that into an exception. Bail early — there is nothing to render to a minimized window anyway, and the restore will fire another resize with real numbers. |
| `OMSetRenderTargets(0, ...)` before disposing | This is the invisible second reference. The context still has your RTV bound to the output merger from the last `OnRender`, and D3D11 holds a reference for as long as it's bound. Binding **0** targets unbinds it. Skip this and `ResizeBuffers` returns `DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL` (`0x887A0001`). |
| `default(ComPtr<ID3D11RenderTargetView>)` in a local | The overload takes the array of views by reference, so it needs *something* to point at even when the count is 0 — a null ComPtr is the idiomatic filler. You can't pass `ref renderTargetView` here; you're about to dispose it, and reading as if it were the live binding is confusing. |
| `renderTargetView.Dispose()` | Your reference. Both this and the unbind are required — releasing one is not enough. |
| `ResizeBuffers(0, ...)` — buffer count | `0` means **keep the current buffer count**. Yours is 2 (from `SwapChainDesc1`). Passing `0` means this line doesn't silently disagree with the descriptor if you ever change it to 3 for triple buffering. |
| `Format.FormatUnknown` | Means **keep the current format** (`FormatB8G8R8A8Unorm`). Resize is not the place to change pixel format; saying "unknown" is DXGI's idiom for "don't touch it." |
| Trailing `0` — flags | `DXGI_SWAP_CHAIN_FLAG` bitfield. You created the swap chain with no flags, and **the flags here must match what you created it with**. Zero in, zero here. (Tearing support in **1.8** adds a flag, and that's the moment this argument stops being zero — it has to be changed in both places or `ResizeBuffers` fails.) |
| `SilkMarshal.ThrowHResult` | `ResizeBuffers` returns an `HRESULT`, not an exception. Ignore it and a failed resize leaves you with a disposed RTV and an access violation on the next frame. Same treatment as every other COM call in `OnLoad`. |
| Recreate the view *after* the resize | `ResizeBuffers` destroys and reallocates the textures. The buffer at index 0 after the call is a different resource than before; the old view described memory that no longer exists. |
| `MinDepth = 0f`, `MaxDepth = 1f` | The depth range the viewport maps NDC z into. `0..1` is D3D's full range and the only sensible default. (Other values are for tricks like forcing a first-person weapon to render in front of the world — a 8.2 concern.) |
| `RSSetViewports(1, in viewport)` | `RS` = rasterizer stage. The `1` is a count: D3D11 supports up to 16 simultaneous viewports for geometry-shader-routed multi-view rendering. You want one. |
| `Width`/`Height` are `float` | Not a typo on D3D's part — viewports are floating point so you can offset by half-pixels. `uint` widens silently, so nothing to do. |

> **Where the resize event actually lands.** `OnFramebufferResize` runs on the same thread that pumps the window, in between `OnRender` calls — never *during* one. That's the only reason it's safe to yank the render target out from under the device context here. Remember that when threading shows up later.

## Step 4 — make it observable

The viewport is invisible today, and a solid-color clear looks identical whether you resized correctly or not. So put the numbers where you already put numbers. Edit **`ReportPerformance`**:

```csharp
private void ReportPerformance()
{
	var framesPerSecond = rendersSinceLastReport / secondsSinceLastReport;
	var avgMilliseconds = secondsSinceLastReport / rendersSinceLastReport * 1000.0;
	var peakMilliseconds = peakRenderSeconds * 1000.0;
	var aspectRatio = (float)backBufferWidth / backBufferHeight;

	window.Title =
		$"Firmament - {framesPerSecond:F0} FPS | {avgMilliseconds:F2} ms avg | {peakMilliseconds:F2} ms peak | {updatesSinceLastReport} updates | {backBufferWidth}x{backBufferHeight} @ {aspectRatio:F2}:1 | {resizeCount} resizes | Background Pauses {pauseBackgroundSwitch}";
}
```

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| `(float)backBufferWidth / backBufferHeight` | The cast is load-bearing. Both fields are `uint`; without it you get integer division and 1280/720 reports as `1.00`. Cast the *numerator* — casting the result is too late. |
| `resizeCount` | Proves the callback fired even when the picture didn't move. You'll want it in ten seconds — see the Checkpoint. |

## ✅ Checkpoint

- Window opens, colors cycle, title bar now reads `... | 1280x720 @ 1.78:1 | 0 resizes | ...`.
- **Maximize** (or double-click the title bar). The size and aspect jump to your monitor's resolution immediately, and `resizes` ticks up. Restore — it goes back.
- **Drag a window border.** Two things happen, and the second one is the lesson:
  - The picture **freezes** while you hold the mouse down. That is not your bug. Dragging a border on Windows enters a modal message loop that owns the thread, so `OnRender` doesn't get called — the title bar is stale because nothing is running to update it.
  - Release the mouse, and the readout snaps to the new size **with `resizes` having jumped by dozens**. GLFW delivered a resize callback for every intermediate size *during* the frozen drag, and each one did a full `ResizeBuffers`. The engine was working the whole time; only the render loop was suspended.
- **Minimize, wait, restore.** No exception. That's the `size.X <= 0` guard earning its keep — remove it and the minimize throws.
- ESC / gamepad Start still quit, SPACE / gamepad A still toggle the cycle. Nothing you built in 1.6 regressed.
- The colors themselves look exactly as before. Correct — a full-screen clear can't show you a viewport, and won't until **2.6**.

## 🐛 Common failure modes

1. **Exception on the very first resize, HRESULT `0x887A0001` (`DXGI_ERROR_INVALID_CALL`).** Something still references the back buffer. Check *both* releases: the `OMSetRenderTargets(0, ...)` unbind **and** `renderTargetView.Dispose()`. Doing only one is the usual mistake, and it fails the same way as doing neither.
2. **Exception the moment you minimize** (HRESULT `0x80070057`, `E_INVALIDARG`). The zero guard is missing or the comparison is `< 0` instead of `<= 0`. A minimized window reports 0×0, not negative.
3. **`AccessViolationException` in `OnRender` right after a resize.** The RTV was disposed but never recreated — either `ResizeBuffers` threw and you swallowed it, or `CreateRenderTargetView()` isn't being called after the resize. There is no managed exception to catch here; a dangling COM pointer takes the process down.
4. **Everything looks blurry after you maximize, and never sharpens.** The event isn't wired. Confirm `window.FramebufferResize += OnFramebufferResize;` is in the constructor (before `Run()`, which is why it goes with the others) and that `resizes` in the title is actually incrementing. If the counter stays 0, you're seeing DXGI stretch the original 1280×720 buffer.
5. **Aspect reports `1.00` at every size.** Integer division — you need `(float)backBufferWidth / backBufferHeight`, cast on the numerator.
6. **Title shows the right size but you're convinced nothing else changed.** Nothing else *did* change. `ClearRenderTargetView` clears the whole resource and ignores the viewport entirely. This is the one lesson in Part 1 whose payoff is deferred; it cashes in at 2.6.
7. **`resizes` climbs forever with no input.** You went and did exercise 4 (aspect locking) and built a feedback loop: your handler sets `window.Size`, which fires another resize, which sets the size again. See the exercise.

## 🔧 Exercises

1. **Watch the two measurements.** Subscribe to `window.Resize` as well and log both sizes side by side. On Windows they'll agree exactly — that's the point. Now know that on a Retina Mac the framebuffer would be 2× the window, which is why the swap chain is fed from `FramebufferResize` and not the other one. (Delete the logging before you commit, per the logging rules.)
2. **Break it on purpose.** Comment out `renderTargetView.Dispose()` and resize. Note the exact HRESULT. Restore it and comment out the `OMSetRenderTargets(0, ...)` line instead — resize again. Same HRESULT, different missing reference. That's twenty seconds that will save you an hour when the depth buffer joins the party in 8.2 and becomes a *third* thing to release.
3. **Letterbox the viewport.** Write a `SetLetterboxedViewport` that fits a fixed 16:9 rectangle inside whatever the window currently is, centered — pillar bars on the sides when the window is too wide, letterbox bars top and bottom when it's too tall. Log the `TopLeftX/Y/Width/Height` you compute and check the arithmetic by hand at a few window sizes. **It will have zero visual effect until 2.6** — you're writing it now because the math is the whole point of the viewport and it's easier to reason about before there's geometry to blame.
4. **Lock the aspect ratio.** In the handler, force `window.Size` back to the nearest 16:9 pair instead of resizing to what was asked for. It will fight you — your write fires another resize event, which writes again. Fix it with a re-entrancy guard, then decide whether you'd actually ship this. (You wouldn't. Now you know why every game letterboxes instead.)
